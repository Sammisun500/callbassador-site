A no-heat call at 9 p.m. and a leaking water heater on a Sunday do not wait for the office to open. The homeowner has a problem in the house. They called the company whose number they already have. If nobody answers, they do not pause the leak while you finish dinner. This guide is about what those calls look like for a small plumbing or HVAC shop, what outside research actually measured, and a practical way to take the details without promising that a technician will show up on a clock.

The call does not only come in at lunch

ServiceTitan published Call Trends in the Trades from its own customer accounts, covering May 1, 2025 through July 31, 2026. In that sample, 10.7% to 12.1% of calls arrived outside normal business hours. The page describes that share as nearly 1 in 8 calls, after the crew has gone home or on the weekend. ServiceTitan says the figures are an aggregated, de-identified sample of its customers, not a census of every trades business in the country. They are still useful if you have ever assumed the phone goes quiet at 5.

The same page says shops with 10 or fewer technicians booked 30% of calls, while shops with more than 50 technicians booked 58%. That gap is descriptive of the sample. It does not prove that a small shop will book more jobs by buying any particular tool. It does match the way a small shop actually works: the owner is often the person who would have answered, and that person is on a job, driving, or done for the day.

What "nobody answered" means in the research

Invoca's Home Services Lead Conversion Benchmarks Report, published July 2026, says 52% of callers to home services businesses speak with a person. The same report says 52% of calls are answered by a person. For calls lasting longer than 15 seconds, which filters out misdials and very short hangups, the answer rate in that report is 65%. For calls over 30 seconds, it is 73%. Answer rates by sub-industry in the report range from 32% to 74%.

The report is not saying those other calls all became voicemails you can return in the morning. It says they did not reach a person. Among calls a person did answer, Invoca reports that 38% were leads and that 45% of those leads converted on the call. That is about conversations that already connected. It is not a forecast for an unanswered after-hours call.

What the caller does while you are off the clock

You cannot see the next tap on their phone. You can see the situations that repeat. A pipe is actively leaking, or the house has no heat, and the caller wants a person who can tell them what to do while they wait and whether someone can come. A maintenance tune-up or a "how much to replace the unit" call can wait until morning more often than a burst supply line can. Both still start as a phone call to whoever is listed.

Voicemail is not a conversation. Some callers leave a name and a number. Many do not stay on the line long enough to do that, which is why a greeting that only says "leave a message" loses the address, the symptom, and whether anyone is standing in water. Even a complete voicemail still waits until you listen to it. The emergency and the quote request look the same in a missed-call list until you play the recording.

Stopping the job you are on to answer is the other default. It works once. It does not work when you are driving, under a house, or asleep. A spouse or an office manager who is not on the clock cannot cover every night unless that is actually their job, with a wage and a schedule. That hire is a real option, and it is a different cost. The comparison is in answering service, AI receptionist, or office manager.

A practical way to handle the after-hours call

Keep the number customers already use. Forward either the calls you do not pick up, the calls that arrive after your posted hours, or every call. The menu path depends on the carrier or the VoIP host. The goal is the same: the caller reaches a voice that can follow your rules, and you get a written summary instead of a blank missed call. Step-by-step forwarding, without a promised star code that would be wrong for some carriers, is in how to forward calls and keep your number.

Decide the emergency list before the first live night. A supply-line leak, no heat below a temperature you choose, a gas smell, or a sparking panel are different from "the AC is noisy." Write what Sami, an AI receptionist, should say, who gets the text, and whether the call should transfer to a cell. Do not script a callback in a fixed number of minutes. The person on call may be 40 minutes away or done for the night. The honest line is that the details were sent and someone from the business will follow up. The caller can be told how to shut a valve or leave the house if that matches your rules and the situation. Arrival time is yours to give when you call them back.

Separate the information you are willing to say out loud. Service area, hours, whether you service a brand, and whether you quote a diagnostic fee are stable. A price you have not approved should not be invented on the call. Booking onto a calendar, on plans that include it, still should wait for your approval before it is treated as a confirmed visit.

What "handled" looks like the next morning

A useful after-hours record has the caller name, a callback number, the address, what they said is wrong, and whether your emergency rule matched. You can return calls in the order you choose: active water, no heat with someone elderly at home, then quotes. You are not reconstructing the night from a list of timestamps with no notes.

Customers already have your number. It is on the truck, the invoices, the website, and the Google listing. You do not need to replace that number to have someone, or an AI receptionist, answer when you cannot. You forward the calls you choose. This is the plain version of how that works for a small home-service business, what to test, and what not to promise the caller.

Forwarding is a rule on the number you already own

Call forwarding tells the phone company or your VoIP host: when a call hits this number, and a condition is true, send it to a second number. The caller still dialed you. Your caller ID, your website, and your magnets stay as they are. The second number is the one the answering service or the AI receptionist picks up. You can turn the rule off later without recalling every magnet you ever printed.

There are three useful conditions.

  • Missed calls only. Your phone rings first. If you do not answer, the call forwards. You still take the calls you can grab between jobs.
  • After hours. During posted hours the call rings your phone. Outside those hours it forwards. The schedule lives either in the carrier's settings or in a phone system that knows your hours.
  • All calls. Every call forwards. Use this when nobody on the crew should be the first ring, including during the day.

You can change the condition later. Shops often start with missed calls, listen for a week, and then add after-hours or the reverse.

Where you turn it on

The taps are not the same on Verizon, AT&T, T-Mobile, a copper landline, and a VoIP host such as a system from your internet provider. Some mobile plans put forwarding under phone settings, in a menu named call forwarding or conditional forwarding. Some business lines do it in a web admin page. Some still use a star code, and the code is not universal. A code that means "forward when busy" on one carrier can mean something else, or nothing, on another. Do not punch in a code from a forum post and assume the nights are covered.

The reliable sequence is: open the admin page or the phone settings for the business line, find forwarding, enter the destination number your receptionist provider gave you, pick the condition, save, and then test. If you cannot find the setting, call the carrier's business support or the company that hosts the VoIP line and ask them to set "forward no-answer," "forward on a schedule," or "forward all," using those words. Write down what they changed.

If the number is a cell phone that is also your personal line, think twice before forwarding all calls. Missed-call forwarding keeps your personal answered calls on the handset and sends the rest. A separate business line is cleaner, and you still do not have to print a different number if the business line is the one customers already use.

What to test before customers hit it

Place three calls from a phone that is not the business line.

  1. During the day, with the handset available, confirm the call rings you when that is what you wanted.
  2. Let a call go unanswered and confirm it reaches the receptionist, not a carrier voicemail you forgot to disable. Carrier voicemail often answers first and blocks forwarding if the ring time is short. Extend the ring time, or turn that mailbox off, if test calls never leave your phone.
  3. Call after your posted hours, if you chose an after-hours rule, and confirm the path changed.

On the answered test, listen for a recording notice at the start. Pennsylvania and other all-party-consent states expect people on the call to know it may be recorded. Callbassador plays that notice on every call. Confirm the greeting uses your business name, that an emergency you defined produces a text to the right mobile, and that a routine question gets the answer you approved. Confirm the AI receptionist does not quote a price you did not approve and does not promise when a technician will call back or arrive.

Hang up and check the summary. You want the caller name, a callback number, the address, and the problem. If any of those are missing, fix the questions before real customers use the line.

Who is answering, said out loud

If the voice is AI, callers should be able to hear that. Sami, Callbassador's AI receptionist, identifies as AI when asked and does not claim to be human. A live answering service should not pretend to be your employee sitting in your office if the script says otherwise. Either way, the caller is trying to get help from your company. The summary still comes to you. You decide the callback.

Emergency rules belong in the setup, not in the caller's imagination. "Water is spreading" can text you and, if you want, transfer to a cell. A fixed arrival time does not belong in the script. You may be across town. The caller can be told you have the details and that the business will follow up.
